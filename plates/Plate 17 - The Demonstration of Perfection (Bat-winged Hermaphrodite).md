# Plate 17: The Demonstration of Perfection (Bat-winged Hermaphrodite)

## Image
A bat-winged hermaphrodite in royal vestments stands on a three-headed monster that eats itself. It holds a snake in its left hand and a chalice with a three-headed snake in its right. Background: a hillside, a bird in a nest pecking at its breast with two young catching drops of blood (pelican), a lion, and a tree with six pairs of sun-heads and a single sun-head on top.

## Text and motto
No verse or motto cited in the sources consulted.

### Etymology and symbolism
- Chthonic: Greek khthonios, 'of the earth', from khthon, 'the earth'; in English from 1882, 'of the underworld' ([Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/chthonic)). Henderson reads the crowns on the snakes' heads as 'the making conscious, spiritually, of the chthonic element'.
- Pelican: Late Latin pelecanus, from Greek pelekan; Etymonline records the belief that pelicans feed their young with their own blood, known in English by about 1200, and says it 'properly belong[s] to some other bird' ([Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/pelican)). Glasgow reads the bird feeding its young on this plate as cibation and as Christ's self-sacrifice.
- Etymologies are from the sources linked here. Where a plate source (ARAS, Glasgow, Henderson) uses the term, the link to the plate is that source's; whether any source draws a symbolic connection from the word's origin itself has not been checked.

## Reading (Henderson, second series)
Joseph L. Henderson, presentation of 18 March 1990 on the second half of the series, summarised by Daniel S. Benveniste (ARAS Connections 2023, Issue 2).

- Henderson: the bat-wings show the monstrous, horrific nature of the work; the crowns on the snakes' heads are 'the making conscious, spiritually, of the chthonic element'; alchemy redeems earthly life by keeping its chthonic nature so spirit and nature do not split.

## Jung

## Other witnesses and notes
- Compare Plate 10.
- Glasgow caption: 'The Demonstration of Perfection - a riot of alchemical imagery with Lion, Sun Tree, Pelican and Three-headed Serpent'; the pelican read as cibation and as Christ's self-sacrifice; the three serpents devouring each other as mutual penetration of body, soul and spirit.
- Adam McLean, commentary in the Magnum Opus edition of the Rosary of the Philosophers (Edinburgh, 1980), hosted on alchemywebsite.com. Layer 3, one author's view. McLean reads all twenty woodcuts as one pattern (Body, Soul and Spirit) and presents his reading as one interpretation among others. What he says about this plate specifically has not yet been read. Full entry: [[Plate 1 - The Mercurial Fountain|Plate 1: The Mercurial Fountain]].
- Wikipedia: woodcuts 10, 17 and 19 have direct precedents in the Buch der heiligen Dreifaltigkeit (Book of the Holy Trinity), an early 15th-century German alchemical treatise attributed to the Franciscan Frater Ulmannus; Wikipedia gives its earliest manuscript (Berlin) as 1410-1419, with a revision of 1433 for Johannes von Bayreuth, and says some of that manuscript's drawings reappear in later 16th-century works including the 1550 Rosarium.
- Glasgow numbering problem: the exhibition page attaches both the 'Sun Tree' detail and the title 'demonstration of perfection' to illustration 15 as well as 17; see [[Plate 15 - Falling Dew on the Hermaphrodite|Plate 15: Falling Dew on the Hermaphrodite]] for the full list.
