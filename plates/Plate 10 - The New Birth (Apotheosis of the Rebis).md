# Plate 10: The New Birth (Apotheosis of the Rebis)

## Image
The Rebis, right side male and left side female, stands on the moon (the vas hermeticum) and has wings for volatility. In one hand it holds a chalice with three snakes (or one three-headed snake), in the other a single snake. At left a raven; at right the sun-and-moon tree (arbor philosophica). Glasgow MS Ferguson 210 shows a moon tree and bird, and a winged figure.

## Text and motto
Verse (English rendering quoted by ARAS): "Here is born the Empress of all honour / The Philosophers name her their daughter / She multiplies / bears children ever again / They are incorruptibly pure and without any stain". Jung also introduces a German poem of about 1550 on the nature of the Hermaphroditus (not transcribed here).

## Alchemical stage
Final stage of the first series: apotheosis of the Rebis; Glasgow reads it as mastery of the lunar element (White Stone)

### Etymology and symbolism
- Hermaphrodite: Greek hermaphroditos, a mythological name for a son of Hermes and Aphrodite; in Ovid's version the nymph Salmacis prayed to be joined with him and their bodies merged into one ([Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/hermaphrodite)). The Rebis of this plate has a male right side and a female left side (ARAS).
- Rebis: Latin res bina, 'dual or double matter'; Wikipedia presents it as the end product of the magnum opus, the initial state of matter, or both, and as a symbol of the union of opposites such as male and female, body and spirit, sun and moon ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rebis)). That article does not mention the Rosarium.
- Etymologies are from the sources linked here. Where a plate source (ARAS, Glasgow, Henderson) uses the term, the link to the plate is that source's; whether any source draws a symbolic connection from the word's origin itself has not been checked.

## Jungian reading (Psychology of the Transference, CW16 ¶525-537)
Section summaries follow the ARAS concordance; lettering is ARAS's.

- (a)-(b) The denarius, the result of the completed work, is the filius philosophorum, used as a Christ-symbol; the culminating point, extended only by multiplication.
- (c)-(d) The Hermaphroditus; the androgyne as an anima figure born of the opus, reflecting the timelessness of the unconscious.
- (e) Man contains a replica of the world (microcosm), corresponding to the collective unconscious.
- (f) 'Immortality' as the alchemists' great hope: not provable as an idea, but felt as an experience tied to the Self. (f-2) The Stone, standing for the Self, drives the work.
- (g) The opus ends with a highly paradoxical being; wholeness can only be described in antinomies.
- (h) Apotheosis of the Rebis: snakes, moon and tree allude to Maria's axiom, the Trinity and the Mercurial serpent. (h-2)-(h-4) The hermaphrodite as a problem in itself; alchemical figures as creatures of night, poison and evil; mythic archetypes later reduced to theories of sexuality.
- (i)-(j) The cleavage in man and the world; wholeness resolving all opposition, the coincidentia oppositorum identified by Cusanus with God.

## Other witnesses and notes
- End of Jung's commentary: plates 11-20 are not discussed in the Psychology of the Transference.
- Edward F. Edinger, The Mystery of the Coniunctio (Inner City Books, 1994; lectures of 19-20 October 1984). Layer 2, a Jungian analyst's reading following Jung. The OFJ listing gives the chapter title for this position in the sequence of ten Rosarium pictures as "Resurrection of the united eternal body" (item 10 of 10). The match to this plate is by position in that listing, and the title differs from the title used on this page; neither the book nor its plate captions have been consulted. Full entry: [[Plate 1 - The Mercurial Fountain|Plate 1: The Mercurial Fountain]].
- Adam McLean, commentary in the Magnum Opus edition of the Rosary of the Philosophers (Edinburgh, 1980), hosted on alchemywebsite.com. Layer 3, one author's view. McLean reads all twenty woodcuts as one pattern (Body, Soul and Spirit) and presents his reading as one interpretation among others. What he says about this plate specifically has not yet been read. Full entry: [[Plate 1 - The Mercurial Fountain|Plate 1: The Mercurial Fountain]].
- Betsy Cohen, IAJS conference paper, April 2022 (Duquesne Scholarship Collection). Layer 3, one author's view. A general argument that Jung chose the Rosarium drawings as a template for the analytic process, shaped by his relationships with Spielrein, Moltzer and Wolff; it is not a reading of this plate. Full entry: [[Plate 1 - The Mercurial Fountain|Plate 1: The Mercurial Fountain]].
- Glasgow detail: the hermaphrodite stands on a crescent moon with a moon tree and a bird, and is winged to emphasise spiritual progress; a detail is labelled 'the Moon Tree'. Glasgow groups illustrations 5-10 as the lunar cycle.
- Wikipedia: woodcuts 10, 17 and 19 have direct precedents in the Buch der heiligen Dreifaltigkeit (Book of the Holy Trinity), an early 15th-century German alchemical treatise attributed to the Franciscan Frater Ulmannus; Wikipedia gives its earliest manuscript (Berlin) as 1410-1419, with a revision of 1433 for Johannes von Bayreuth, and says some of that manuscript's drawings reappear in later 16th-century works including the 1550 Rosarium. Wikipedia describes woodcuts 10 and 17 as depicting the 'Hermetic androgyne'. Whether the precedent images match closely has not been checked.
