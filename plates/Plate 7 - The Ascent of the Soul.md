# Plate 7: The Ascent of the Soul

## Image
The soul rises from the decaying corpse (a male soul figure, per ARAS summary). Glasgow MS Ferguson 210, illustration 7, titled "The Extraction or Impregnation of the Soule", adds a tree growing from the central figure's abdomen, an addition not in the original text.

## Text and motto
Verse (English rendering quoted by ARAS): "Here is the division of the four elements / As from the lifeless corpse the soul ascends."

## Alchemical stage
Putrefactio; nigredo; iterum mori; cauda pavonis (ARAS)

## Jungian reading (Psychology of the Transference, CW16 ¶475-482)
Section summaries follow the ARAS concordance; lettering is ARAS's.

- (a) The nature of the soul is a function of relationship: a bond between two parts merged into one, leaving the body for its heavenly source.
- (b) The dark state of disorientation: breakdown of elements as dissociation and collapse of ego-consciousness; latent psychoses may emerge.
- (c) Conscious mind liable to submersion; compared to 'loss of soul' in primitive peoples; yoga and levitation reports.
- (d) Corpse as residue of the past, the man who is no more; the 'torments' belong to reiterated death.
- (e) Required treatment: the analyst keeps his own orientation; symbolic, experience-based understanding.
- (f) Therapy aims at strengthening the conscious mind; (f-1) harmful when the conscious attitude already suppresses the unconscious; (f-2) the unconscious invasion like the Nile flood; the nigredo compared with St John of the Cross's dark night.
- (g) Modern intellect strayed too far into consciousness; cauda pavonis colours signal renewal.
- (h) The homunculus becoming the filius regius, the soul on its way to the undivided First Man (Anthropos).
- (i) The idea of mystery forces itself on the inquirer; archetype of the birth of the divine child.

## Names and works cited
Hermes, Tabula smaragdina, St John of the Cross, Basilides ('third filiation'), Physis, Mercurius, Sapientia Dei, Anthropos.

The OFJ listing gives the chapter title for this position in the sequence of ten Rosarium pictures as "Separation of soul and body" (item 7 of 10). The match to this plate is by position in that listing, and the title differs from the title used on this page; neither the book nor its plate captions have been consulted. A general argument that Jung chose the Rosarium drawings as a template for the analytic process, shaped by his relationships with Spielrein, Moltzer and Wolff; it is not a reading of this plate.
