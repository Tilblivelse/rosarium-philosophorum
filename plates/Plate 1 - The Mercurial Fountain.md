# Plate 1: The Mercurial Fountain

## Image
A square with a star in each corner (the four elements) and a fifth star above the centre (the quinta essentia). Below, a circular basin, the vas Hermeticum, holds the mare nostrum or aqua permanens; six stars on its rim, with Mercurius, make the seven planets/metals. A fountain rises from the basin through three pipes labelled lac virginis, acetum fontis and aqua vitae. Above the pipes stand the sun and moon. At the top a two-headed serpent, the serpens mercurialis, spits fire.

## Text and motto
Opening verse (in the English rendering quoted by ARAS): "We are the metals' first nature and only source". The verse continues with lines on the fountain's healing and poisonous powers; the remainder is not transcribed here (to enter from the 1550 print).

## Alchemical stage
Prima materia; Mercurius as source (no single operation named by ARAS)

### Etymology and symbolism
- Quintessence: Medieval Latin quinta essentia, 'fifth essence', a rendering of Greek pempte ousia ([Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/quintessence)). In ancient philosophy and medieval alchemy the fifth essence was the ether that Aristotle added to earth, water, air and fire, a prized substance for alchemists to extract. On this plate the fifth star above the centre is the quinta essentia (ARAS).
- Mercury: Latin Mercurius, the Roman god and messenger of Jupiter, possibly from merx, 'merchandise'; the planet took the god's name and the metal, known to alchemy, took the name later; its Greek name hydrargyros means 'liquid silver' ([Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/mercury)). On this plate Mercurius stands in the basin with the six rim stars to make the seven planets and metals, and the serpens mercurialis crowns the fountain (ARAS).
- Matter and prima materia: Latin materia, 'substance from which something is made' and also the hard inner wood of a tree, linked by scholars to mater, 'origin, source, mother'; Greek hyle, 'wood', gave Aristotle's term for matter ([Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/matter)). Wikipedia describes the prima materia as the primitive formless base of all matter, and quotes the Theatrum Chemicum as saying it begets, conceives and gives birth to itself ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prima_materia)). Glasgow MS Ferguson 210 captions this plate as the fountain representing alchemy's Prima Materia.
- Etymologies are from the sources linked here. Where a plate source (ARAS, Glasgow, Henderson) uses the term, the link to the plate is that source's; whether any source draws a symbolic connection from the word's origin itself has not been checked.

## Jungian reading (Psychology of the Transference, CW16 ¶402-409)
Section summaries follow the ARAS concordance; lettering is ARAS's.

- (a) The basin: the vas Hermeticum as matrix uniting the hostile elements of the square; Mercurius as pater metallorum, a unity of the seven planets.
- (b) Three manifestations of Mercurius: the fountain as a triad, the chthonic counterpart of the heavenly Trinity.
- (c) The divided serpent: serpens bifidus as the duplex natura of Mercurius; linked to the 'duo fumi' of Maria the Copt.
- (d) Axiom of Maria: the process as 4 > 3 > 2 > 1, from chaos to the filius regius.
- (e) Odd and even numbers: odd as masculine, even as feminine.
- (f) Quaternity as an ordering schema; the circle as completeness.
- (g) Triad as agens, dyad as patiens.
- (h) The picture as the unconscious psyche projected into matter; (h-1) Goethe's Faust; (h-2) Chymical Wedding of Christian Rosencreutz (1616) as forerunner of Faust with the same Axioma Mariae.
- (i) 'Water' of the Art signifies the unconscious (synonyms: medicina catholica, aqua Saturni, vinum ardens).
- (j) Gushing up and flowing back completes a circle; linked to Nicholas of Cusa's image of God.

## Names and works cited
Dorn, Maria the Copt, Nicholas of Cusa, Dante, Goethe (Faust), Chymical Wedding (1616); Jung refers onward to 'The Spirit Mercurius'.

## Other witnesses and notes
- Glasgow MS Ferguson 210 (18th-c. English copy): the three substances are labelled Lac Virginis, Acetum fontis, Aqua Vitae, mixing in the basin to form the water of Mercury. MS Ferguson 6 (17th-c. German) labels the cherub-headed streams Spiritus, Anima, Corpus.
- The earliest dated manuscript witness claimed for the Rosarium (Michael Cochem, colophon 1529, Inlibris listing) has the fountain on f. 13, described with three liquids, a six-pointed star, sun, moon and a two-headed dragon. Dealer claim; Wikipedia states no manuscript predates the 1550 print.
- Modern dissenting reading (blog essay, Benoit Rousseau, 7 March 2025, gnosisjung.org): argues Jung's reading 'does not make sense' (no explanation of why chaos is linked to four; the three faucets and the 'inorganic world' in humans left unidentified) and proposes that the four elements correspond to four cognitive functions, the sun to the dominant function, the moon to the three undeveloped ones, and the quintessence to all four operating; compares Greek four-part schemes (temperaments, humours) and Myers-Briggs typing. This is one author's interpretation, not established scholarship. The essay also lists Mercurius's alternate names: mare nostrum, aqua permanens, mare tenebrosum. It quotes Jung (CW16 ¶404) on the 'tetrameria' of the transforming process and the 'pluralistic state of the man who has not yet attained inner unity'.
- Later readings of the series (not documentary witnesses; plate-specific content for plate 1 has not yet been read in the originals):
  - Edward F. Edinger, The Mystery of the Coniunctio: Alchemical Image of Individuation (Inner City Books, 1994; Studies in Jungian Psychology by Jungian Analysts, no. 65; edited by Joan Dexter Blackmer; from lectures at the C.G. Jung Institute of San Francisco, 19-20 October 1984). Layer 2, a Jungian analyst's reading following Jung. The OFJ listing describes it as an introduction to Jung's Mysterium Coniunctionis together with a psychological interpretation of the ten Rosarium pictures, and gives the first chapter as "The mandala fountain". The book itself has not been consulted here.
  - Adam McLean, commentary in his Magnum Opus edition of the Rosary of the Philosophers (Edinburgh, 1980), now hosted on alchemywebsite.com. Layer 3, one author's view. McLean reads the twenty woodcuts as a single integrated pattern, a process uniting Body, Soul and Spirit and mirroring physical alchemy and inner soul development. He describes his method as synthesis rather than intellectual analysis and presents his reading as one interpretation among possible others. He also states that Jung showed only 11 of the 20 illustrations.
  - Betsy Cohen, "How Jung's Erotic Relationship with Three Ex-Patients led him to the Rosarium Drawings, his template for healing in psychoanalysis" (IAJS conference paper, April 2022, Duquesne Scholarship Collection). Layer 3, one author's view. Cohen treats the woodcuts as Jung's chosen template for the analytic process, with analyst and patient cast as the King and Queen of the drawings, and argues that Jung's relationships with Sabina Spielrein, Maria Moltzer and Toni Wolff help explain why he chose them. She also criticises Jung for tending to separate the archetypal and the personal psyche. This is a general argument about the series, not a reading of plate 1.
- Printed reprises (Wikipedia): the Artis auriferae (Basel, 1572) reproduced the twenty illustrations as re-cut woodcuts; Johann Daniel Mylius, Philosophia reformata (1622), includes the twenty images redesigned in early 17th-century style by Balthazar Swan.
- Manuscript witnesses (Wikipedia's list; none is said to predate the 1550 print): 14 of the late 16th century, among them Glasgow MS Ferguson 6, BL Sloane MS 2560, BL Add MS 29895, Bodleian MS Ashmole 1487, Paris BnF MS Lat. 7171, Vatican MS Reg. Lat. 1278, Leiden MS Vossianus Chym. F. 12 (dated 1575) and Rome, Lincei MS Verginelli-Rota 6 (dated 1597); 5 of the 17th century (including Glasgow MS Ferguson 96 and 149, Getty Center MS 232); 5 of the 18th century (including Glasgow MS Ferguson 210, 29 and 74, Getty Center MS 49); and 4 of the 19th century (including Wellcome Library MS 1091 and 4256). The list is Wikipedia's; the manuscripts themselves have not been consulted. This conflicts with the Cochem 1529 dealer claim above.
- Other Glasgow manuscripts named on the Glasgow exhibition page: MS Ferguson 29 (French, 16th c., 20 black-and-white drawings); MS Ferguson 74 (French, 16th c., 21 pen-and-ink drawings); MS Ferguson 96 (Latin, 16th c., Frankfurt illustrations pasted in and coloured); MS Ferguson 149 (German, 16th c., 21 illustrations, mostly drawn within flasks). Glasgow dates MS Ferguson 210 to the 18th century, English, with 20 coloured miniatures pasted in, derived with some alteration from the printed version. Dating differs between the two sources: Wikipedia's list places Ferguson 29 and 74 in the 18th century and Ferguson 96 and 149 in the 17th, while Glasgow's page gives all four as 16th century.
- Translations (Wikipedia): two excerpts were translated into English in the early 17th century by a pseudonymous woman, 'likely Jeanne du Port' in Wikipedia's wording; an 18th-century English translation based on Glasgow MS Ferguson 210 reproduces its coloured drawings as woodcuts; a Czech translation of 1578 by Jaroš Griemiller is held in the National Library in Prague (line drawings). The alchemywebsite.com text transcribes the English translation of MS Ferguson 210.
- Attribution and date of the text (two sources disagree): ARAS (Benveniste) names the author as Petrus Toletanus of Toledo, mid-1200s (the ARAS text spells the name 'Toltetanus' as read here; check the spelling in the original); Wikipedia names no author and says no manuscript predates the 1550 print. Neither claim has been checked against a scholarly edition.
- Karen-Claire Voss, "The Hierosgamos Theme in the Images of the Rosarium Philosophorum", in Proceedings of the International Conference on the History of Alchemy, ed. Z.R.W.M. von Martels (Brill, Leiden, 1990). Cited by Wikipedia; layer 3, a scholarly reading of the series through the hierosgamos theme. Not consulted.
- ARAS (Benveniste) also cites Johannes Fabricius, Alchemy: The Medieval Alchemists and their Royal Art (1976), and Jung, The Practice of Psychotherapy (1954; CW16, pp. 241-245, 247, 250, 273). What those passages say about the Rosarium has not been read.
